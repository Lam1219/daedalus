Daedalus: getting unstuck, on the watch people already wear

Daedalus is a freezing-of-gait companion for people with Parkinson's, on Apple Watch and Wear OS with their phones. This proposal focuses the first release on one job, getting unstuck. It also sets out how a three-week home pilot will test that job before anything else is built.

Words used on this page

R1, R2, R3 are the three releases. R1 is the first app people can get, with the beat started by a press. R2 adds freeze monitoring under a Health Canada licence. R3 adds automatic cueing after a controlled trial. The silent detector is the freezing detector already on both watches. It never cues; it only records what it would have done, so its accuracy can be measured at home. The false-alarm gate is the test automatic cues must pass first: few enough wrong cues per hour at home. Class I is Health Canada's lowest-risk medical device class.

  • 1One job first. The person presses "I'm stuck" and a beat plays at their own walking pace, then stops once they're walking. Around it: setting their pace with their physio, a weekly view, and doses and last night's sleep shown beside the times they got stuck. Breathing is included because it is already built.
  • 2The beat is played where it works best. It can play on the wrist, as sound in earbuds, or as vibration from the phone in a front trouser pocket. No study has tested the wrist alone, and every positive vibration result cued nearer the legs or trunk.
  • 3A home pilot before more building. Most freezes last 1–10 seconds, so the key question is whether a press-started beat arrives before the freeze is over. Twelve people who freeze use the prototype at home for three weeks, and every press is measured.
  • 4A Class I device in Canada, not a wellness app. A stepping beat sold to people with Parkinson's for freezing is unlikely to pass as wellness in either country. The plan is to ask Health Canada to classify it.
  • 5A small first market. About 1 in 5 older adults wears a smartwatch. That puts roughly 9,000 Canadians who freeze within reach without new hardware. The interviews will measure the real share.

People in Canada with Parkinson's who freeze, already wear an Apple Watch or Galaxy Watch, and see a neurological physiotherapist. 4 · Value proposition and value curve

One press, your own pace, where you'll feel or hear it

Who

People with Parkinson's who freeze and already wear a smartwatch.

Why

Their feet stick at doorways, turns and when they start walking. They feel embarrassed and avoid shops.

What before

Their own tricks, which fade; a laser cane that stays at home; nothing at all outdoors.

How

One press on the watch starts a beat at their own walking pace: on the wrist, in their earbuds, or from the phone in their pocket. It stops by itself once they are walking.

What after

They get going again quietly, in public too. They and their care team can see when they got stuck, next to their doses and how they slept.

Alternatives

Own tricks, laser cane, metronome app, Parky (not in Canada), CUE1+ (unavailable).

Value curve

0 = none, 4 = strong, judged from sections 1 and 2. Daedalus's line is the R1 target, not a measurement; the home pilot measures it.

5 · Recommendation

Three directions; we recommend A

Recommended

A · The freeze loop

For people who freeze and own a watch, reached through their physio. Daedalus does one job well: get unstuck.

The first version does
  • Start the beat with one press, from the app, a watch-face complication or a control.
  • Play it on the wrist, in earbuds, or from the phone in a front trouser pocket, at the person's pace. It stops when they walk.
  • Remind them to pocket the phone when they stand up and it isn't coming along.
  • Set the pace with a one-minute walk, with the physio.
  • Show the week, and doses and last night's sleep beside the presses, shareable with the physio and neurologist. It never suggests a dose.
  • Log each dose at the time it was actually taken.
  • Breathing exercises, which are already built.
It deliberately leaves out
  • Automatic cues (the silent detector only records), detected symptoms in the dose report (R2), dyskinesia and posture.
  • The Fine motor and Calm vibration modes (patterns for hand tasks and for calming) are available behind a setting, with no tremor claim.

Trade-off: less to show a clinic or investor, and more riding on one feature.

B · A physio's home-practice tool

For neuro physiotherapists: they prescribe walking practice with a beat, the watch delivers sessions, and they see who keeps it up.

  • Fits better under an FDA policy that leaves alone apps helping people manage their own condition, such as home exercise.
  • Doesn't depend on pressing a button mid-freeze.
  • Clinics pay, but they score lowest on pain and willingness to pay.

Trade-off: it helps practice, not the freeze in the doorway.

C · A broad Parkinson's companion

Several cue modes, a tremor check, breathing, medication, the dose report and dyskinesia, all at once.

  • The most features.
  • Competes head-on with Parky and StrivePD, using the same Apple algorithm.
  • No evidence that a wrist buzz calms tremor.

Trade-off: no specific, provable reason for anyone to choose it.

Why A

Freezing is where the pain is, and a search of app stores, the Parkinson's UK Tech Guide and the news found nothing on the watch for freezing in Canada. A is largely built, so the pilot that decides it is cheap to run. Its outcome is uncertain: if people can't start the beat in time, the trigger changes (section 9). B's physio set-up is kept inside A as the way people start. C spreads the product thin where Parky already sits.

Weakest consequential assumption: H1

If someone who is frozen can't start the beat, or it only arrives once a short freeze has ended on its own, a press-started cue fails exactly when it matters. The recommendation would then move to cues that start before a freeze (on standing up, or for the first seconds of every walk), and bring automatic detection forward.

10 · Next steps

From here to the first participant

  • A study build of both apps is ready for the pilot. It is behind a study switch that's off by default, and press files stay on the devices until the physio exports them. It still needs checks on real devices: haptics, earbuds, pocket vibration and battery over a day.
  • Partner, consent and confirmation. Sign a private physiotherapy practice, write the consent sheet, and get the regulatory consultant's written confirmation (Class I; no ethics board for the pilot).
  • Interviews start straight away (CAD 5–10k).
  • The pilot itself: recruit 12 people, one clinic visit each, three weeks at home, then analysis. About 11 weeks in all: 3 to set up, 6 with participants at home in overlapping groups, and 1–2 for analysis.
  • Afterwards, the results come back to this page, and the R1 build follows what they show.
